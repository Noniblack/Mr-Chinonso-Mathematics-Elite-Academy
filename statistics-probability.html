Foundational

Lesson 1: Data, sampling and frequency distributions

Data are collected observations or measurements. Quantitative data are numerical; qualitative data describe categories. Discrete data are countable, while continuous data can take values within an interval.

Primary data are collected directly for a purpose. Secondary data have already been collected by another person or organisation. A sample is a subset of a population.

Frequency tables

A frequency tells us how often a value or class occurs. The total of all frequencies is the total number of observations.

Foundational

Lesson 2: Graphs and data representation

  • Bar chart: compares categories, usually with separated bars.
  • Pie chart: represents a whole as sectors of a circle.
  • Histogram: represents continuous grouped data with adjacent bars. When class widths differ, use frequency density.
  • Frequency polygon: joins plotted points at class midpoints.
  • Cumulative frequency curve (ogive): plots cumulative frequency against class boundaries and helps estimate medians, quartiles and percentiles.
Pie-chart angle = (category frequency ÷ total frequency) × 360°
Frequency density = frequency ÷ class width
Worked example: pie chart

In a group of 40 learners, 10 choose Biology as their favourite subject.

Sector angle = (10 ÷ 40) × 360° = 90°.

The Biology sector should measure 90°.

Common error: In a histogram with unequal class widths, bar height is frequency density, not raw frequency. Use the convention specified in the question.
Foundational

Lesson 3: Mean, median, mode and range

Mean = sum of observations ÷ number of observations
Mean from a frequency table = Σfx ÷ Σf

Median is the middle value after arranging the data in order. For an even number of observations, average the two middle values.

Mode is the most frequently occurring value. A data set may have more than one mode or no unique mode.

Range = maximum value − minimum value
Worked example

Find the mean, median, mode and range of 2, 4, 4, 6, 9.

  1. Mean = (2 + 4 + 4 + 6 + 9) ÷ 5 = 25 ÷ 5 = 5.
  2. Median = middle value = 4.
  3. Mode = most frequent value = 4.
  4. Range = 9 − 2 = 7.

Grouped data

For grouped data, use class midpoints as representative values when estimating the mean.

Estimated grouped mean = Σ(f × class midpoint) ÷ Σf

For grouped median or mode, use the appropriate grouped-data formula only when it is part of the prescribed syllabus. These results are generally estimates because the original observations are not known exactly.

Lesson 4: Variance and standard deviation

Measures of dispersion describe how spread out the observations are. Two groups can have the same mean but different variability.

Variance measures the average squared deviation from the mean. Standard deviation is the square root of the variance and is expressed in the same units as the original data.

Population variance: σ² = Σ(x − μ)² ÷ N
Population standard deviation: σ = √[Σ(x − μ)² ÷ N]
Shortcut for population variance: σ² = Σx² ÷ N − μ²
These formulas use the population convention, dividing by N. Some courses also teach sample variance, which divides the sum of squared deviations by n − 1. Follow the definition required by the question.
Worked example

Find the population variance and standard deviation of 2, 4, 6.

  1. Mean = (2 + 4 + 6) ÷ 3 = 4.
  2. Deviations from the mean: −2, 0, 2.
  3. Squared deviations: 4, 0, 4. Their sum is 8.
  4. Variance = 8 ÷ 3 = 8/3 ≈ 2.67.
  5. Standard deviation = √(8/3) ≈ 1.63.

A smaller standard deviation generally indicates that observations lie closer to the mean. A larger standard deviation indicates greater spread, but comparisons should consider the units and scale of the data.

Lesson 5: Correlation and Spearman's rank correlation

Correlation describes the direction and strength of an association between two variables. Positive association means that larger values of one variable tend to accompany larger values of the other; negative association means that larger values of one tend to accompany smaller values of the other.

A scatter diagram helps reveal the pattern. Correlation alone does not prove that one variable causes the other.

Spearman's rank correlation coefficient

Spearman's rank correlation assesses how closely two sets of ranks are associated. It is useful when data are ranked or when a monotonic relationship is being investigated.

For ranks without ties: rₛ = 1 − [6Σd² ÷ n(n² − 1)]

Here, d is the difference between the two ranks for each person or item, and n is the number of paired observations.

Worked example: ranking two sets

Suppose four learners have ranks in Mathematics and Science as follows:

LearnerMath rankScience rankdd²
A1100
B23−11
C3211
D4400

Σd² = 2 and n = 4.

rₛ = 1 − [6(2) ÷ 4(4² − 1)] = 1 − 12/60 = 0.8.

This indicates a strong positive rank association in this small example.

The simple formula above assumes there are no tied ranks. When ties occur, assign average ranks and use the method prescribed by the relevant syllabus or course; do not apply the no-ties formula uncritically.

Pearson's product-moment correlation is a different measure, commonly used to describe linear association between numerical variables. Do not confuse it with Spearman's rank correlation.

Foundational

Lesson 6: Fundamental probability

A sample space is the set of all possible outcomes. An event is a collection of outcomes from that sample space.

For equally likely outcomes: P(A) = number of outcomes in A ÷ total number of outcomes
P(not A) = 1 − P(A)
P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)
If A and B are independent: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)
Worked example: a fair die

A fair six-sided die is rolled once. Find the probability of obtaining a number greater than 4.

Sample space = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. Favourable outcomes = {5, 6}, so P(number greater than 4) = 2/6 = 1/3.

Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together in the same trial. Independent events are events for which the occurrence of one does not change the probability of the other. These terms are not interchangeable.
Syllabus-dependent Advanced applications

Lesson 7: Counting principles, permutations and combinations

Counting methods help determine the number of possible arrangements or selections without listing every possibility.

n! = n × (n − 1) × ... × 2 × 1, with 0! = 1

A permutation is an arrangement in which order matters. A combination is a selection in which order does not matter.

Permutations: ⁿPᵣ = n! ÷ (n − r)!
Combinations: ⁿCᵣ = n! ÷ [r!(n − r)!]
Worked example: selecting a team

How many different groups of 3 learners can be selected from 5 learners?

Order does not matter, so use a combination.

⁵C₃ = 5! ÷ [3! × 2!] = (5 × 4) ÷ (2 × 1) = 10 groups.

Worked example: awarding positions

In how many ways can first, second and third positions be awarded among 5 learners, with no ties?

Order matters, so use a permutation.

⁵P₃ = 5! ÷ 2! = 5 × 4 × 3 = 60 arrangements.

How to choose the method

  • Use permutations when changing the order creates a different outcome.
  • Use combinations when the same selected group counts only once, regardless of order.
  • Check for restrictions, repetition, identical objects or circular arrangements before applying a formula.
Syllabus-dependent Advanced applications

Lesson 8: Conditional probability and multi-stage events

Conditional probability is the probability of event A occurring when it is known that event B has occurred.

P(A | B) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B), provided P(B) > 0

Tree diagrams help organise multi-stage experiments. Multiply probabilities along a path, then add the probabilities of separate paths that satisfy the event being investigated.

Worked example: drawing without replacement

A bag contains 3 red balls and 2 blue balls. Two balls are drawn at random without replacement. Find the probability that both are red.

  1. Probability the first ball is red = 3/5.
  2. After a red ball is drawn, 2 red balls remain among 4 balls. Probability the second is red = 2/4.
  3. Probability both are red = (3/5) × (2/4) = 3/10.

Connecting counting and probability

When outcomes are equally likely, permutations or combinations can count favourable outcomes and total outcomes. Ensure that the favourable and total outcomes are counted using the same method.

Worked example: combination-based probability

A committee of 2 learners is selected at random from 5 learners, of whom 3 are girls and 2 are boys. Find the probability that both selected learners are girls.

  1. Total committees = ⁵C₂ = 10.
  2. All-girl committees = ³C₂ = 3.
  3. Probability = 3/10 = 0.3.
Advanced probability, Spearman's rank correlation and formal permutation/combination techniques are extensions whose exact depth depends on the examination board and course. Learn them when required by your current syllabus or next academic pathway.

Question 1 — Mean and median (Foundational)

Find the mean and median of 3, 5, 5, 7, 10.

Solution: Mean = (3 + 5 + 5 + 7 + 10) ÷ 5 = 30 ÷ 5 = 6. The middle value is 5, so the median is 5.

Question 2 — Pie charts (Foundational)

There are 60 learners in a club. If 15 belong to the drama group, find the angle of the drama sector in a pie chart.

Solution: (15 ÷ 60) × 360° = 90°.

Question 3 — Variance (Examination / syllabus-dependent)

Find the population variance of 1, 3, 5.

Solution: Mean = 3. Squared deviations are 4, 0 and 4. Population variance = (4 + 0 + 4) ÷ 3 = 8/3 ≈ 2.67.

Question 4 — Spearman's rank correlation (Advanced)

For 5 paired observations with no tied ranks, Σd² = 4. Find Spearman's rank correlation coefficient.

Solution: rₛ = 1 − [6(4) ÷ 5(5² − 1)] = 1 − 24/120 = 0.8.

Question 5 — Permutations or combinations? (Advanced)

How many different ways can 2 representatives be selected from 6 learners?

Solution: Order does not matter, so use a combination: ⁶C₂ = 6! ÷ (2!4!) = (6 × 5) ÷ 2 = 15.

Question 6 — Probability without replacement (Advanced)

A bag contains 4 green balls and 3 yellow balls. Two balls are drawn without replacement. Find the probability that both are green.

Solution: (4/7) × (3/6) = 12/42 = 2/7.
